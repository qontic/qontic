About Q-Ontic

Why we present the alternatives of quantum mechanics.

Mission

Quantum mechanics predicts the results of experiments with extraordinary accuracy. As usually taught, it does not say what happens between measurements, or what makes a measurement different from any other physical process. Students learn to calculate, and the question of what is physically happening is set aside.

Yet precise physical theories exist that answer it. Pilot-wave theory, collapse models and many worlds each state what exists and how it evolves. Physics education research has shown repeatedly that skill in calculation does not by itself produce understanding.

Q-Ontic's aim is to bring these alternatives into the teaching of quantum mechanics, so that a physical picture can come before the calculation and guide it. We think students are owed that.

John Bell, whose theorem grew out of his study of pilot-wave theory, asked why that theory was ignored in textbooks. He argued that it should be taught, “not as the only way”, so that students see that the vagueness of the standard account is a choice and is not forced by experiment.1 We take the same view of every serious alternative.

What you will find here

Simulations, notebooks and videos that show the same experiment as the textbook describes it and as each alternative describes it. They are organized by physics topic.

For now, our comparative simulations cover three views: Orthodox, Pilot-Wave, and Many-Worlds. We may add further views in the future, including spontaneous-collapse models.

Frequently asked questions

Where does the textbook approach fit?

It is included: we call it the Orthodox view. Our comparative simulations present it alongside pilot-wave theory and many worlds, so students can explore how each describes the same experiment. The Orthodox view uses the familiar textbook account of wave-function evolution, measurement probabilities, and collapse at measurement. Comparing these descriptions brings their shared predictions and different physical accounts into focus, including questions about what constitutes a measurement.2

Do the alternatives make different predictions?

The three views currently presented in our comparative simulations reproduce the same standard quantum predictions for the experiments shown. Spontaneous-collapse models, which we may include in the future, do make different predictions: they modify the dynamics so that superpositions break down spontaneously, with stronger effects for larger systems. Experiments are searching for these departures from standard quantum mechanics.3

If the predictions agree, why does it matter?

Because physics is more than predicting numbers. A picture of what is happening guides intuition and suggests new questions. Bell found his theorem by asking whether the nonlocality of pilot-wave theory could be avoided.4 Deutsch's proposal of a universal quantum computer was motivated by many worlds.5 Collapse models have led to new experimental tests of quantum mechanics.3

Which one is right?

Nobody knows. Q-Ontic does not tell students what to conclude. The aim is that they know the options exist, what each one claims, and which problems remain open.

Is this a Bohmian mechanics project?

No. Several people in the team have worked on Bohmian mechanics, and at present more of the material covers it, partly because particle trajectories are easy to show. The project is not committed to it. Contributions on other approaches are welcome.

Does this replace the standard course?

No. Students still need to calculate, and nothing here replaces that. The resources are meant to be used alongside a standard course: a short demonstration in class, a homework exploration, or a starting point for discussion.

What does physics education research say?

That students can solve standard problems without understanding the physics behind them.6 In quantum mechanics specifically, what students come to believe about the meaning of the theory depends on whether and how the instructor addresses the question. Leaving it out does not leave them without a view.7

How can I use these resources, or contribute?

The resources on this site are free to use in teaching. The source code is on GitHub. If you use the material in a course, find an error, or want to contribute, write to yepes@rice.edu.

How does this connect to questions about the meaning of quantum mechanics?

Q-Ontic starts with concrete physics: experiments, equations, and simulations. Comparing the Orthodox view, pilot-wave theory, and many worlds also raises questions about what the wave function describes and how measurement outcomes arise. These questions connect physics with philosophy of science. Our aim is to make the different accounts accessible through physical examples, so students can examine their assumptions and form their own judgments.
