Q-Ontic’s Mission

Bringing different views into quantum mechanics teaching.

Mission

Quantum mechanics predicts the results of experiments with extraordinary accuracy. The usual textbook approach describes wave-function evolution and gives rules for measurement outcomes, but it leaves open how one definite outcome arises from that evolution. Students learn to calculate, while questions about what is physically happening receive less attention. Physics education research shows that skill in solving numerical problems does not by itself establish conceptual understanding.1

Pilot-wave theory and many worlds are two ways to address these questions, and there are others, such as spontaneous-collapse models. Q-Ontic's aim is to bring these views into the teaching of quantum mechanics, alongside the orthodox view, so that a physical picture can come before the calculation and guide it. We think students are owed that. We begin with pilot-wave theory and many worlds, and we welcome work on other views.

John Bell, whose theorem grew out of his study of pilot-wave theory, asked why that theory was ignored in textbooks. He argued that it should be taught, “not as the only way”, so that students see that the vagueness of the standard account is a choice and is not forced by experiment.2 We take the same view of every serious alternative.

What you will find here

Simulations, notebooks and videos that show how different views describe the same experiment. They are organized by physics topic.

For now, our comparative simulations cover three views: Orthodox, Pilot-Wave, and Many-Worlds. We plan to add further views, including spontaneous-collapse models.

Frequently asked questions

Where does the textbook approach fit?

It is included: we call it the Orthodox view. Our comparative simulations present it alongside pilot-wave theory and many worlds, so students can explore how each describes the same experiment. The orthodox view uses the familiar textbook account of wave-function evolution, measurement probabilities, and collapse at measurement. Comparing them shows where the predictions agree and where the descriptions differ, including on what counts as a measurement.3

What do the three views say?

Orthodox: The wave function evolves according to the Schrödinger equation between measurements. Measurement probabilities follow the Born rule; on measurement, the wave function collapses to a state corresponding to the recorded outcome.

Pilot-Wave: Particles have definite positions, and the guidance equation determines their motion from a wave function that obeys the Schrödinger equation. A measurement is an interaction involving the system and apparatus, with the actual configuration determining the recorded outcome.

Many-Worlds: The wave function always evolves according to the Schrödinger equation, without collapse. Measurement correlates the system, apparatus, and observer in different branches, each containing a corresponding outcome.

Why these three views, and what about others?

They are where we started. Any view can be added if it says enough about an experiment to be shown in a simulation. We plan to add spontaneous-collapse models, and we welcome proposals and contributions for others.

Do the views make different predictions?

The three views currently presented in our comparative simulations reproduce the same standard quantum predictions for the experiments shown. Spontaneous-collapse models, which we plan to include in the future, do make different predictions: they modify the dynamics so that superpositions break down spontaneously, with stronger effects for larger systems. Experiments are searching for these departures from standard quantum mechanics.4

If the predictions agree, why does it matter?

Because physics is more than predicting numbers. A picture of what is happening guides intuition and suggests new questions. Bell found his theorem by asking whether the nonlocality of pilot-wave theory could be avoided.5 Deutsch's proposal of a universal quantum computer was motivated by many worlds.6

Which one is right?

Nobody knows. Q-Ontic does not tell students what to conclude. The aim is that they know the options exist, what each one claims, and which problems remain open.

Is this a Bohmian mechanics project?

No. Several people in the team have worked on Bohmian mechanics, and at present more of the material covers it, partly because particle trajectories are easy to show. The project is not committed to it. Contributions on other approaches are welcome.

Does this replace the standard course?

No. Students still need to calculate, and nothing here replaces that. The resources are meant to be used alongside a standard course: a short demonstration in class, a homework exploration, or a starting point for discussion.

What does physics education research say?

In two modern physics courses, Baily and Finkelstein found that students’ views about quantum systems differed with the instructional approach and varied across contexts.7 Their later synthesis argues that students develop their own views when interpretation is left unaddressed, and presents evidence that explicitly attending to those views can help students develop more consistent descriptions of quantum phenomena.8 These findings motivate our comparative approach; they do not by themselves establish that Q-Ontic improves learning.

How can I use these resources, or contribute?

You can explore the resources online without charge. For copying, adapting, or redistributing a resource, consult its license or contact its author. The source code is on GitHub. If you use the material in a course, find an error, or want to contribute, write to yepes@rice.edu.

How does this connect to questions about the meaning of quantum mechanics?

Q-Ontic starts with concrete physics: experiments, equations, and simulations. Comparing the orthodox view, pilot-wave theory, and many worlds also raises questions about what the wave function describes and how measurement outcomes arise. These questions connect physics with philosophy of science. Our aim is to make the different accounts accessible through physical examples, so students can examine their assumptions and form their own judgments.
