    The setup

    Three spaceships A, B and C float at rest. B and C are a distance L apart, and A sits halfway between them. A fragile thread is tied from B to C. A sends a light signal; when it arrives, B and C fire identical engines and run identical acceleration programs. Seen from A, the two ships have the same speed at every moment and stay a distance L apart. Does the thread break?

    The short answer, in one frame

    Stay in A's frame, the frame the ships start from. The gap between the ships stays L. The thread does not: a thread moving at speed v is in equilibrium at a shorter length, L/γ, where γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). Held at L, it is stretched by a fraction γ − 1. At 0.9 c, γ = 2.29, so the thread would have to stretch by 129%. A thread that snaps at elongation ε breaks when the ships reach

    vc = c √(1 − 1/(1 + ε)²)

    (0.55 c for ε = 20%). The ships themselves are shorter too, by the same factor. Only the gap is not, because nothing ties the two engines together except the agreed program.

    This is not a matter of how anyone measures the thread. The thread is under tension that a strain gauge on it would record, whoever looks at it.

    Why a moving thread is shorter

    This is Bell's own explanation, and it also works in A's frame. The thread is held together by electric forces between charges. The field of a charge moving at speed v is flattened along the motion by 1/γ. Bell followed one electron orbiting a nucleus that is pushed gently to speed v. Using Maxwell's field equations, the Lorentz force and Lorentz's relativistic relation between momentum and velocity, he found that the orbit becomes an ellipse shorter by 1/γ along the motion, and that it goes round more slowly by the factor γ. Matter held together by such forces settles at a shorter length when it moves.

    The Atoms of the thread option under Display is only a schematic of this.

    Bell adds a proviso: the contraction is what happens to a bound object accelerated gently, so that its internal forces keep it near equilibrium. A strong thread tied to light ships would instead pull the ships together, and nothing would break. Here the ships are taken to be far too massive for the thread to move them.

    The same result in the ships' frame

    Choose + Ships' frame. The lower panel is the momentary rest frame of the rear ship B. There the ships did not start together: the front ship C started first and pulls ahead. When both coast, they are at rest relative to each other a distance γL apart, and the thread, of rest length L, is stretched by γ − 1. Same number, different story.

    Why γL: two objects moving together at speed v that are a distance L apart in A's frame are γL apart in their own frame. This is length contraction read the other way round, since A sees their own separation γL contracted to L. The clocks (Display) show where the extra distance comes from: in B's frame C's clock reads ahead of B's by γvL/c² once both coast, so C fired first.

    In spacetime

    The spacetime diagram plots the worldlines in A's frame, time upward, light at 45°. The horizontal dashed line joins events that happen at the same time for A; the gap along it stays L. The tilted line joins events at the same time for B; the gap along it grows. The shaded band is the thread's history, and the red star marks the break.

    Changing the front engine

    Contraction by itself breaks nothing: the ships contract too, and nobody expects them to break. The thread breaks because the gap between its ends does not shrink with it.

    The slider Hold the front ship back (k) sets the front ship's proper acceleration to a/(1 + k·aL/c²). With a gentler front engine C gains speed a little more slowly, and B closes in. Once both coast at speed v, the gap in A's frame is L − kL(1 − 1/γ), and the thread is stretched by

    (γ − 1)(1 − k).

    Only k = 1 leaves the thread exactly at its natural length L/γ: the gap shrinks with it, and in the ships' frame the distance stays L. This is Born-rigid motion (Nikolić, Ref. 4 under Rationale). It is a special tuning, not something that happens by itself: below k = 1 the thread is still stretched and can snap; above it the ships end closer than the thread's natural length and it goes slack.

    What k = 1 does with engines, a strong thread would do on its own if the ships were light: pull them into exactly this motion. That is Bell's proviso.

    What the model assumes

    • Units with c = 1. Lengths in units of L, times in units of L/c.
    • Each ship has a constant proper acceleration until it reaches the final speed, then coasts. The default acceleration is gentle (aL = 0.2 c²).
    • The thread has no mass and does not pull on the ships. Its elongation is computed as in Bell's argument: in A's frame, the gap divided by the natural length L/γ at the ships' speed. In Bell's case this is exactly γ − 1 at every moment. With a gentler front engine the two ships briefly have different speeds and the mean speed is used; the error is below 2.5%, so differences under 3% are shown as taut.
    • The ships' frame and the spacetime diagram show the distance in the rear ship's momentary rest frame. It equals γL once the ships coast; during the acceleration it is somewhat larger, because in that frame the front ship is ahead in its program.
    • The break is drawn at the middle of the thread. The recoil of the two halves is schematic.

    Bell's question at CERN

    John Bell described this problem in 1976, in an essay on how to teach special relativity. He put it to a distinguished experimental physicist, who refused to accept that the thread breaks and thought Bell had misread relativity. They took it to the CERN Theory Division. An informal canvass there produced, by Bell's account, a clear consensus that the thread would not break. Bell added that many who first answer wrongly reach the right answer with some thought, often by working out how things look to B or C.1 The same effect had been analysed earlier by Dewan and Beran.2,3

    Why it belongs here

    Bell remarked that people with a classical education, who know the work of Larmor, Lorentz and Poincaré, tend to have better instincts here.1 Einstein's route starts from the principle that the laws look the same to every uniformly moving observer, and gets contraction and time dilation from it quickly and in full generality. The older route, which Bell called the longer road, works in one frame and asks how the forces inside moving matter change its shape and its rhythms. Bell judged that the facts of physics do not force either philosophy, and that the longer road gives students a feel for the subject that the shorter one can skip.1 The thread question is a case in point: it is asked in one frame, about one object, and that frame answers it directly.

    The dynamical account has its own limits. Brown and Pooley point out that Bell's argument shows contraction follows if the laws that hold matter together have Lorentz symmetry; it does not explain why they do, and the stability of matter needs more than classical electromagnetism.5 Which account counts as the explanation is still debated by philosophers of physics.

    Why it belongs on Q-Ontic

    Here two accounts agree on every prediction and tell different stories about what is happening, each making some questions easy and others hard. The formulations of quantum mechanics are in a similar position, with one difference: there, some proposals, such as spontaneous-collapse models, do predict different results and are being tested.
