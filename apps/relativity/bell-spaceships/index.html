    The setup

    Three spaceships A, B and C float at rest. B and C are a distance L apart, and A sits halfway between them. A fragile thread is tied from B to C. A sends a light signal; when it arrives, B and C fire identical engines and run identical acceleration programs. Seen from A, the two ships have the same speed at every moment and stay a distance L apart. Does the thread break?

    The short answer, in one frame

    Stay in A's frame, the frame the ships start from. The gap between the ships stays L. The thread does not: a thread moving at speed v is in equilibrium at a shorter length, L/γ, where γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). Call this its relaxed length at that speed; its rest length is still L. Held at L, it is stretched by a fraction γ − 1. At 0.9 c, γ = 2.29, so the thread would have to stretch by 129%. A thread that snaps at elongation ε breaks when the ships reach

    vc = c √(1 − 1/(1 + ε)²)

    (0.55 c for ε = 20%). The ships themselves are shorter too, by the same factor. Only the gap is not, because nothing ties the two engines together except the agreed program.

    This is not a matter of how anyone measures the thread. The thread is under tension that a strain gauge on it would record, whoever looks at it.

    Why a moving thread is shorter

    This is Bell's dynamical explanation, given in A's frame. (Whether this or the geometry of spacetime is the deeper explanation is debated; see Rationale.) The thread is held together by electric forces between charges. The field of a charge moving at speed v is flattened along the motion by 1/γ. Bell followed one electron orbiting a nucleus that is pushed gently to speed v. He took Maxwell's field equations, the Lorentz force and Lorentz's relativistic relation between momentum and velocity, and described what a computer integration of them gives: the orbit becomes an ellipse contracted along the motion by the factor √(1 − v²/c²), and it goes round more slowly, its period longer by the factor γ. He then used the Lorentz invariance of these equations to show that the same holds for any system of charges set gently in motion. Matter held together by such forces settles at a shorter length when it moves.

    The Atoms of the thread option under Display is only a schematic of this.

    Bell adds a proviso: the contraction is what happens to a bound object accelerated gently, so that its internal forces keep it near equilibrium. A strong thread tied to light ships would instead pull the ships together, and nothing would break. Here the ships are taken to be far too massive for the thread to move them.

    The same result in the ships' frame

    Choose + Ships' frame. The lower panel is the momentary rest frame of the rear ship B. There the ships did not start together: the front ship C started first and pulls ahead. When both coast, they are at rest relative to each other a distance γL apart, and the thread, of rest length L, is stretched by γ − 1. Same number, different story.

    Why γL: two objects moving together at speed v that are a distance L apart in A's frame are γL apart in their own frame. This is length contraction read the other way round, since A sees their own separation γL contracted to L. The clocks (Display) show where the extra distance comes from: in B's frame C's clock reads ahead of B's by γvL/c² once both coast, so C fired first.

    In spacetime

    The spacetime diagram plots the worldlines in A's frame, time upward, light at 45°. The horizontal dashed line joins events that happen at the same time for A; the gap along it stays L. The tilted line joins events at the same time for B; the gap along it grows. The shaded band is the thread's history, and the red star marks the break.

    Changing the front engine

    Contraction by itself breaks nothing: the ships contract too, and nobody expects them to break. The thread breaks because the gap between its ends does not shrink with it.

    The slider Hold the front ship back (k) sets the front ship's proper acceleration to a/(1 + k·aL/c²). With a gentler front engine C gains speed a little more slowly, and B closes in. Once both coast at speed v, the gap in A's frame is L − kL(1 − 1/γ), and the thread is stretched by

    (γ − 1)(1 − k).

    Only k = 1 leaves the thread exactly at its relaxed length L/γ: the gap shrinks with it, and in the ships' frame the distance stays L. This is Born-rigid motion (Born, Ref. 7; Nikolić, Ref. 4; both under Rationale). It is a special tuning, not something that happens by itself: below k = 1 the thread is still stretched and can snap; above it the ships end closer than the thread's relaxed length and it goes slack. ("Only" refers to this family of engine programs; other programs, such as a delayed start for C, can also end with no stretch.)

    What k = 1 does with engines, a strong thread would do on its own if the ships were light: pull them into approximately this motion, after some elastic jostling. That is Bell's proviso: “A sufficiently strong thread would pull the rockets together… But if the rockets are too massive to be appreciably accelerated by the fragile thread, the latter has to break” (Ref. 1, p. 75).

    What the model assumes

    • Units with c = 1. Lengths in units of L, times in units of L/c.
    • Each ship has a constant proper acceleration until it reaches the final speed, then coasts. The default is aL = 0.2 c². Whether acceleration is gentle for a real thread also depends on its elastic response.
    • The thread does not pull on the ships. We prescribe the motion of each piece, using the same family of acceleration programs as the ships. This illustrates the stretching without solving the thread's elastic response. The exact snap time is therefore a result of this simplified model, not a prediction for a particular material.
    • A small piece moving at speed v and spanning dx in A's frame has proper length γ dx. Its stretch is this length divided by its initial length, minus 1. The thread breaks when any piece reaches the chosen limit. In Bell's case all pieces stretch equally by γ − 1; with a gentler front engine the rear end reaches the limit first. Results show the greatest stretch. At k = 1 every piece stays unstretched. Above k = 1 we draw a slack thread schematically; the slider is limited so that the ships and the prescribed pieces cannot pass one another.
    • When the pieces move at different speeds, there is no single relaxed length in A's frame. The app then shows the greatest stretch and both ships' speeds.
    • The ships' frame and the spacetime diagram show the distance in the rear ship's momentary rest frame; its scale is in L, measured in that frame from B. In Bell’s case it equals γL once the ships coast; during the acceleration it is somewhat larger, because in that frame the front ship is ahead in its program. The gap at the snap is taken along B's line of simultaneity through the break, which meets B's path earlier for a midpoint break, or at the break itself for a rear-end break.
    • In Bell’s case the break is drawn at the middle, since every part reaches the limit together. Otherwise it is drawn at the rear end, where the limit is reached first. Recoil after breaking is schematic.

    Bell's question at CERN

    John Bell described this problem in 1976, in an essay on how to teach special relativity. He put it to a distinguished experimental physicist, who refused to accept that the thread breaks and thought Bell had misread relativity. They took it to the CERN Theory Division. An informal canvass there produced, by Bell's account, a clear consensus that the thread would not break. Bell added that many who first answer wrongly reach the right answer on further reflection, usually by working out how things look to B or C, and accept it “perhaps only with a residual feeling of unease”, although the conclusion is “perfectly trivial in terms of A's account of things, including the Fitzgerald contraction” (p. 68).1 The same effect had been analysed earlier by Dewan and Beran.2,3

    Why it belongs here

    Bell's impression was that “those with a more classical education, knowing something of the reasoning of Larmor, Lorentz, and Poincaré, as well as that of Einstein, have stronger and sounder instincts” (p. 68).1

    He saw two differences between Lorentz's approach and Einstein's. One is philosophy. Lorentz held that there is a real state of rest, defined by the aether, which the laws of physics hide from experiment; Einstein declared “really resting” and “really moving” meaningless. In Bell's words, “The facts of physics do not oblige us to accept one philosophy rather than the other. And we need not accept Lorentz's philosophy to accept a Lorentzian pedagogy.” The other is style. Einstein starts from the hypothesis that the laws look the same to all observers in uniform motion, which gives “a very concise and elegant formulation”; the older route starts from the laws in one frame and works out what happens to moving matter and moving observers. Bell made “no reservation whatever about the power and precision of Einstein's approach”, but added: “The longer road sometimes gives more familiarity with the country” (p. 77).1

    The thread question is a case in point: it is asked in one frame, about one object, and that frame answers it directly. Bell's pedagogy needs no aether: A's frame is used only because the question is asked there, and the same reasoning works in any inertial frame.

    Brown and Pooley turn Bell's pedagogy into a claim about explanation: rods contract and clocks slow because the laws governing matter are Lorentz covariant, and Minkowski's geometry codifies that symmetry rather than explaining it. They also stress what this assumes: that all the laws holding matter together share the Lorentz symmetry of Maxwell's equations, and that stable rods and clocks need a quantum theory of matter, not classical electromagnetism.5 The rival, geometrical view takes the structure of spacetime itself, as described by Minkowski, to be what explains why rods and clocks behave this way; Janssen gives a careful statement of it and a critique of the dynamical account.6 Which account counts as the explanation is still debated by philosophers of physics.

    Why it belongs on Q-Ontic

    Lorentz's account (as completed by Poincaré) and Einstein's agree on every prediction and differ on what explains them, each making some questions easy and others hard. The app's two frames are not those two accounts: both frames belong to one theory, and either account can use either frame. Interpretations of quantum mechanics are in a similar position to Lorentz's and Einstein's accounts, with one difference: some rivals, such as spontaneous-collapse models, are distinct theories with different predictions, now being tested.
