    The apparatus

    A beam splitter divides a light pulse into two halves. One runs along arm 1, the other along arm 2, at right angles. Both arms have length L when the apparatus is at rest. Each half reflects from a mirror and comes back to the splitter, where the two halves are recombined and watched as interference fringes. If the halves arrive together, the fringes sit in one place; if one half is late by a time Δt, the fringes move by cΔt/λ, where λ is the wavelength. The simulation draws each half as a single dot so the arrival times can be seen directly.

    The 1887 expectation

    Suppose light moves at c through a resting ether and the apparatus moves through it at speed v, keeping its shape. Along the motion, the light goes out at c − v relative to the apparatus and back at c + v. Across the motion, it must aim slightly ahead and travels a slanted path. With γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²):

    talong = 2Lγ²/c,   tacross = 2Lγ/c,   Δt = 2Lγ(γ − 1)/c ≈ Lv²/c³ for v ≪ c.

    At the exaggerated 0.6 c of the simulation, γ = 1.25 and the exact delay is 0.625 L/c.

    Turning the apparatus by 90° swaps the arms and reverses the sign of Δt, so the fringes should move as it turns. The chart in the corner of the simulation shows the expected delay at each angle: zero at 45°, reversed at 90°. Michelson and Morley folded each light path with mirrors to about 11 m, or 2 × 10⁷ wavelengths. At Earth's orbital speed (v/c ≈ 10⁻⁴) and λ ≈ 550 nm, the expected shift for a 90° turn was 2Lv²/(λc²) ≈ 0.4 of a fringe. They found a shift “certainly less than the twentieth part of this, and probably less than the fortieth part,” consistent with none.1

    The relativistic picture, in two frames

    The views Apparatus moving and Apparatus at rest show the same events in two frames. With the apparatus moving at v, its arm along the motion is shorter by 1/γ and its clock runs slow by 1/γ. With the apparatus at rest, both arms are L, and markers at rest in the other frame stream past, closer together. Lorentz and Einstein both draw these two pictures. They differ in how they read them: for Lorentz one frame is the true rest frame, which he called the ether; for Einstein no frame is special.

    Lorentz's account

    Keep the ether, and suppose that a body moving through it is shorter along its motion by the factor 1/γ. FitzGerald suggested this in 1889,2 and Lorentz independently soon after. Lorentz tied it to the forces that hold matter together: they are electromagnetic, and the field of a moving charge is flattened along the motion. The arm along the motion is then L/γ long, its round trip takes 2(L/γ)γ²/c = 2Lγ/c, exactly the time across. The two halves arrive together at every angle. In the simulation, the dotted outline shows each arm's length at rest.

    Contraction alone explains the null result. Larmor and Lorentz also found that processes in a moving body run slow by 1/γ, so the clock carried by the apparatus reads 2L/c when the halves return, not 2Lγ/c. The slow clocks are needed for the Kennedy–Thorndike experiment of 1932, which used arms of unequal length.3

    Turn the apparatus to 45° in this account and the arms no longer look perpendicular in the frame where the apparatus moves: contraction along the motion changes angles as well as lengths. In the apparatus's own measurements they are still at right angles, because its rulers have contracted too.

    Einstein's account

    In 1905 Einstein took two principles as starting points: the laws of physics are the same for every uniformly moving observer, and light moves at c for each of them.4 There is no ether, and no frame is special. In the apparatus's own frame both arms are L, light moves at c out and back, and each half returns after 2L/c. In a frame in which the apparatus moves, as in the Apparatus moving view, it is shorter and its clock is slow, exactly as Lorentz says. For Einstein these follow from the principles, which also fix how the forces inside the arms must behave; that frame has no special status.

    What the two accounts agree on

    Everything that can be measured in special relativity. In Both, the two panels run on the apparatus clock: the halves leave at 0 and return together at 2L/c in each. If the top frame is the true rest frame, Lorentz's account says that the true time is longer, 2Lγ/c, and that the apparatus clock is slow; no measurement can check that, because on this account the true rest frame cannot be found by any experiment.

    What the model assumes

    • Units with c = 1. Lengths in units of L, times in units of L/c.
    • Speeds are exaggerated so that the effects can be seen. At 0.0001 c the contraction is five parts in a billion.
    • Each half of the pulse is a point; mirrors and splitter are ideal. The light paths are shown, not the fringes.
    • The rotation angle is measured in the apparatus's rest frame. The apparatus moves along its arm 1 direction when the angle is 0°.
    • The 1887 and Apparatus moving panels are drawn in a frame where the apparatus moves at v; the Apparatus at rest panel in its rest frame, where the speed slider only sets how fast the other frame's markers stream past.

    Two accounts, one set of results

    Lorentz's account and Einstein's predict the same result for this experiment and for every other experiment in special relativity; extending Lorentz's view to gravitation takes further assumptions. They differ on what is happening. In Lorentz's, there is a true rest frame, which he called the ether, and the apparatus physically shrinks because the forces inside it change with motion; the true rest frame exists but can never be detected. In Einstein's, no frame is special; contraction and slow clocks follow from the equivalence of all uniformly moving frames.

    Lorentz

    Keeps: one true rest frame (his ether) and one true time.

    Adds: moving bodies shorten along their motion and moving clocks run slow, because the forces inside them change.

    Owes: a reason why no experiment can find the true rest frame. Defenders reply that this follows if all the forces that hold matter together share the same symmetry.5,6

    Einstein

    Keeps: the old principle that uniform motion cannot be detected, now extended to light.

    Adds: light moves at c for every observer; lengths, durations and simultaneity depend on the frame.

    Owes: a physical account of why rods and clocks obey the principles. Defenders reply that the principles are the explanation.

    Why it belongs here

    Einstein's route is the one taught today: it is short and general. John Bell argued that Lorentz's longer road is still worth teaching, because it gives a physical picture of why moving things change, and because the facts do not force a choice between the two.5 Harvey Brown develops the dynamical view at book length.6 The companion app Bell's Spaceships takes Lorentz's road through a question that confuses many physicists.

    Interpretations of quantum mechanics are in a similar position: views that agree on predictions and disagree on what is happening. One difference: some rivals, such as spontaneous-collapse models, are distinct theories with different predictions, now being tested.
