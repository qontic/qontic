1 — What you are looking at

The density displays (Volume, Slices, Lobes, Cloud) show |ψ|² for the chosen hydrogen state.

The moving dots and their trails show pilot-wave (de Broglie–Bohm) theory. Each dot is the electron of a separate hydrogen atom prepared in the same state. Its starting position is drawn at random from |ψ|², and it then moves with the guidance velocity of section 3.

The density |ψ|² is the same in every formulation of quantum mechanics: it gives the probability of finding the electron in a region if its position is measured. The button next to Reset switches between two readings of the display. Orthodox shows only |ψ|²: in the standard (Dirac–von Neumann) reading an electron has a definite position only when its position is measured, so there are no dots or trails, and the Cloud view shows possible outcomes of position measurements on many atoms. The probability current j = (ħ/me) Im(ψ*∇ψ) is also standard quantum mechanics (it circulates about z when m ≠ 0); reading j/|ψ|² as the velocity of an electron that always has a position is the pilot-wave step. Pilot Wave adds the electrons and their trajectories. No measurement takes place in this app, so the two readings agree on everything it shows. Many-worlds and spontaneous-collapse (GRW) theories are not offered separately: an isolated atom does not branch, and a GRW collapse of a single atom happens about once in 3×108 years (rate 10−16 s−1, reference 10), so both would draw the same picture as Orthodox. They read it differently: in many-worlds |ψ|² gives the weights of the branches in a later measurement; in the mass-density version of GRW it is how the electron's matter is spread. Switching does not reset the clock.

Units are atomic units (a₀ = ħ = me = e = 1): lengths in Bohr radii, a₀ = 52.9 pm; one atomic unit of time is 24.2 attoseconds (as). The clock shows attoseconds; 1 fs = 1000 as.

2 — Hydrogen eigenstates

The time-independent Schrödinger equation Hψ = Eψ for hydrogen with a fixed proton has exact solutions labelled by three integers:

ψn,l,m(r,θ,φ) = Rnl(r) · Ylm(θ,φ)
SymbolNameRangeWhat it sets
nPrincipal1, 2, 3…Energy: En = −13.6/n² eV
lAngular0 … n−1Shape: s (sphere), p (two lobes for p₀, px, py; a ring for p₊₁, p₋₁), d, f…
mMagnetic−l … +lOrientation; ψ ∝ eimφ, angular momentum Lz = mħ

Rnl are the associated Laguerre radial functions and Ylm the spherical harmonics (Condon–Shortley phase). An eigenstate has a stationary density. A superposition of states with different energies has a density that beats at the angular frequency ΔE/ħ (period 2πħ/ΔE); for 1s + 2s or 1s + 2p0 the period is 16.8 a.u. = 405 as.

3 — Pilot-wave (de Broglie–Bohm) guidance

In pilot-wave theory the electron has a definite position at all times. Its velocity is set by the wave function:

v = (ħ/me) Im(∇ψ / ψ)

In atomic units v = Im(∇ψ/ψ). For m ≠ 0, ψ ∝ eimφ and the electron circles the z axis at speed vφ = m/(r sinθ), at fixed r and θ. For a real ψ (every m = 0 eigenstate, and real combinations such as px) the velocity is zero: the electron stays where it is. (This holds for the standard guidance law; with the spin term of section 4 it moves.)

If the starting positions are distributed as |ψ|², they stay distributed as |ψ|² at all later times (equivariance), because v = j/|ψ|² with j the probability current of the Schrödinger equation. Given that starting distribution (the quantum-equilibrium hypothesis, which Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì justify by a typicality argument, reference 3), pilot-wave theory gives the same position statistics as standard quantum mechanics.

Why is the 1s electron at rest (standard guidance law)?

In the first-order theory used here no force is needed: ψ is real, so v = 0. Bohm's original second-order formulation (reference 1) gives the same answer in terms of forces. Write ψ = R eiS/ħ. The electron then feels, besides the Coulomb potential V, the quantum potential Q = −(ħ²/2me) ∇²R / R. For 1s, R ∝ e−r gives Q = −1/2 + 1/r (a.u.), so Q + V = −1/2 = E everywhere: the two forces cancel and an electron at rest stays at rest. It does not fall into the proton.

For 1s, the mean kinetic energy ⟨p²⟩/2me = 13.6 eV is what momentum measurements give. In pilot-wave theory a momentum measurement is an interaction that changes the guiding wave; its result is not the velocity the electron had before. The outcomes follow |ψ̃(p)|², as in standard quantum mechanics.

4 — The optional spin term

For a spin-½ electron the wave function is a two-component spinor Ψ = (ψ↑, ψ↓)T with density ρ = |ψ↑|² + |ψ↓|². The continuity equation fixes the probability current only up to a divergence-free term, so a pilot-wave theory has to choose its guidance law. The Spin term button (Advanced) switches between two choices. On an orbital it adds a spin direction together with the spin term. On a spin preset (1s↑ + 2s↓ …) or a j-state it changes only the guidance law and keeps the state, so you can watch the same spinor under both laws:

v = Im(Ψ†∇Ψ) / ρ   (convective; spin off)
v = Im(Ψ†∇Ψ) / ρ  +  (∇×S) / (2ρ)   (with the spin term)

where S = Ψ†σΨ is the spin density and σ the Pauli matrices. Holland argued that the second law is the one fixed by the nonrelativistic limit of the Dirac current; Colijn and Vrscay computed hydrogen trajectories with it (references 4–7).

The added current ½∇×S = ρ·(∇×S)/(2ρ) has zero divergence (the added velocity itself does not), so both laws move |ψ|² in exactly the same way and predict the same position statistics. They give different trajectories. With the spin term, a 2p0 or 1s electron moves even though |ψ|² is stationary. Whether the 1s electron is at rest therefore depends on which guidance law pilot-wave theory adopts; no position measurement distinguishes the two.

1s with a fixed spin direction

For 1s ⊗ |n̂⟩, S = ρ n̂, so ∇×S = ∇ρ × n̂ with ∇ρ = −2ρ r̂:

v = n̂ × r̂    |v| = sin ∠(r̂, n̂)

The electron circles the spin axis with angular speed 1/r (ħ/(mea₀r) in SI units), as found by Colijn and Vrscay. The Verify button (State and diagnostics) compares the app's velocity field with this formula.

5 — Spin states in this app

Product mode

Ψ = ψn,l,m(r) ⊗ |n̂⟩, with |n̂⟩ = cos(θ/2)|↑⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|↓⟩. The θ and φ sliders set the spin direction. Orbital and spin are not entangled.

j-state mode

With spin–orbit coupling the eigenstates are labelled by total angular momentum j = l ± ½ and its projection mj. They are built with Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, for example

|j=3/2, mj=+1/2⟩ = √(2/3)|m=0⟩|↑⟩ + √(1/3)|m=+1⟩|↓⟩

The list offers every (j, mj) for the selected (n, l). The app keeps the nonrelativistic Coulomb energies and does not compute fine-structure splittings. For l = 0 the j-states are pure |↑⟩ or |↓⟩, the same as product mode with θ = 0 or π.

6 — Approximations and numerics

  • Nonrelativistic hydrogen with a fixed proton. No fine structure, no electron–electron interaction.
  • The model includes no coupling to the radiation field.
  • The app omits spin–orbit evolution; with spin–orbit coupling a general product state would evolve into a coupled one. The presets 1s↑ + 2p0↓ and 2p+1↑ + 3d+1↓ are therefore not stationary once fine structure is included.
  • Eigenstates use the exact rotation about z. Superpositions and the spin term use fourth-order Runge–Kutta with finite-difference gradients (h = 0.07 a₀). Speeds above 25 a.u., which occur only very near nodes of ψ, are capped.
  • Cloud samples are exact draws from |ψ|². When the density changes in time the cloud is redrawn each frame, so its dots are not trajectories. With the spin term the cloud's dots follow trajectories (step 0.12 a.u.), and their number is limited to 500.
  • Lobes are drawn where |ψ|² equals a set fraction of its peak (Display: Lobe level), not as the surface that encloses 90% of the probability often drawn in chemistry books, so their size depends on that setting.
  • The Checks panel compares ⟨r⟩ summed over the rendering grid with the exact value ⟨r⟩ = (3n² − l(l+1))/2 for an eigenstate. In 1s + 2s, ⟨r⟩ oscillates in time (the 1s–2s cross term is nonzero); in 1s + 2p0 it does not.
